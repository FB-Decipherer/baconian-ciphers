Today's claim

The only published close-up of Owen's cipher canvas does not show his method. The lines that dominate it — fourteen parallel diagonals crossing the whole frame — carry no colour at all, so they are creases in the fabric, not pencil. One coloured mark survives measurement in the entire image.

findings may be superseded by later reports

The short version

In the 1890s a Detroit physician named Orville Ward Owen claimed that Francis Bacon had hidden a long prose narrative inside the printed works of Shakespeare, Spenser, Marlowe, Peele and Burton — recoverable by collecting every sentence containing certain guide words and reassembling them. To do it he built a machine: two spools in a wooden frame, and between them a thousand feet of canvas with roughly a thousand printed pages pasted to it, so he could wind the whole English Renaissance past his eye and mark it in coloured pencil.

The canvas still exists. The frame and the second spool do not. What survives is in storage in Gardiner, Montana, and there is exactly one published close-up photograph of it, at 300 × 225 pixels.

Today's result in one sentence. That photograph was measured for the first time, and the lines that dominate it — a family of fourteen parallel diagonals crossing the whole frame — carry no colour at all. They are creases in the fabric, not Owen's pencilling. One coloured mark survives measurement in the entire image.

Questions

What is the Word Cipher?

A claimed method, not a code in the ordinary sense. Owen's account is that certain common words — fortune, honour, nature, pan and a handful of others — were planted as signposts, and that the sentences containing them, gathered from across many books and sorted into piles by a shared key word, assemble into a continuous secret narrative.

The procedure he describes is physical and mundane: pencil around every sentence containing the guide word in use, head each sheet with its key word, sort the sheets into piles by key, then join like to like. Nothing about it requires a machine — the machine was for finding the sentences fast in a very large body of text.

What is the cipher wheel, and where is it now?

A wooden cylinder 36 inches across and 48 inches high, wound with one continuous strip of canvas 48 inches wide, with roughly a thousand pages cut from old editions pasted to it and annotated in coloured pencil. It was one half of a reel-to-reel arrangement: cylinder, wooden frame, and a twin empty spool to wind onto.

Virginia Fellows, who wrote about Owen in The Shakespeare Code, donated it to The Summit Lighthouse in Gardiner, Montana, where it is in storage. The frame and the twin spool are gone. They have the canvas; they do not have the machine.

Its condition is the pressing fact. Carla Groenewegen, who published on it in 2017: after more than a hundred years the glue is drying out and some of the pages are crumbling. A canvas in that state cannot be wound and unwound. Whatever is on it can be read once, carefully, or not at all.

What did you actually measure today?

The photograph itself, as an image. Three things.

First, the lines. An edge detector and a line finder were run over it with no human placing anything by hand. Eighty-nine long lines came back. Fifty-four are horizontal and ten vertical — those are the pasted-page joins, the column rules and the edges of the text blocks. Fourteen sit in a tight band around 135°: a family of parallel diagonals, evenly spread across the whole frame, crossing page joins and column rules without regard for either.

Second, whether those diagonals are coloured. Owen's marks were coloured pencil, so a mark should carry colour. Measured against the canvas's own tint, the fourteen diagonals average a chroma of 2.19 — lower than randomly drawn lines at the same angle in the same picture, which average 2.68. The frame's genuinely coloured patches sit at 14.5. The probability that a random line beats them is 0.43. They are, in colour terms, indistinguishable from blank canvas.

Third, a control. The same pipeline was run on three crops of an unmarked, equally dense page of 1623 letterpress, reduced to the same size and compressed the same way. It found 0, 2 and 2 lines. So the detector does not manufacture lines out of dense type — the canvas photograph really does contain long linear structure. That structure just is not ink.

Doesn't that undermine your own project?

It undermines a claim this project had itself made, which is the point of making it public.

An exhibit sheet prepared here in August described this photograph's coloured markings as being "in roughly six colors" and called them "the primary data of the cipher method." The first half does not survive measurement — six colours cannot be recovered from this image, and most of what looks like colour under amplification is compression noise. The second half is true in principle and useless in practice: the coloured markings are the primary data, and this photograph does not contain them.

What the exhibit sheet got right stands: the page architecture is folio, not quarto; the head-title is the Folio's THE TRAGEDIE OF ROMEO and IVLIET; and there is a FINIS with an ornamental tailpiece to its right, marking the close of a play. Those are structural identifications and they hold at this resolution.

Is there any eyewitness that Owen's method worked?

There is one, and he is unusually good.

Josiah Byram Millett — Harvard graduate, founder of a Boston publishing house — went to Detroit in February 1893 and asked Owen to show him. Owen handed him a key word, Story of the Spanish Armada, showed him the procedure, and left him to it. Millett copied about a hundred lines from various parts of the wheel himself and assembled them, adding no words of his own. Owen had independently written out his own version beforehand. The two were compared and, in Millett's account, corresponded almost exactly — the differences being copying slips of his own.

He went back twice. On the third visit, in December 1895, he was let into the workshop and spent several hours there before Owen appeared, going through a pile of some two thousand foolscap sheets unsupervised, and satisfied himself that they contained passages from the Iliad — a judgment he was equipped to make, having read Homer since university.

That is a named, traceable professional performing the operation himself and reporting a match. It is not proof the cipher is real: Millett was a sympathiser, the conditions were not controlled, and a method with enough freedom in it can be taught to produce the expected answer. But it is a great deal better than nothing, and it is the kind of testimony this project goes looking for.

What would a proper scan settle?

Everything today's measurement could not touch. How many colours Owen used, and whether they mean anything. Whether the marks enclose sentences, as his own description says, or underline words, or do something else entirely. Which passages he selected, from which editions, in what order — which is the whole of the method's evidence.

The frame in that photograph covers roughly eight pasted pages of about a thousand, at something like 8,400 pixels per page. A 400 dpi scan of the same area would be near ten million. The gap is not marginal; it is the difference between a rumour of data and data.

A rig for it exists on paper: a gantry that carries the camera over the canvas rather than moving the canvas, a flattening platen that applies only its own weight, and a lighting and colour spec written for faded pencil on aged fabric. CAD through bill of materials is done. It has never been built, because there is nothing to point it at yet.

What would change your mind?

Stated in advance, as every issue of this series does:

  • If a higher-resolution copy of this same photograph shows the diagonals to be coloured after all, today's result is wrong and will be withdrawn here in the same terms.
  • If a scan shows the markings are underlines rather than enclosures, Owen's own description of his procedure is inaccurate, and every reconstruction built on it — including this project's — has to be rebuilt.
  • If the guide-word structure Owen claims turns out to be no denser at his cited sites than anywhere else in the text, the method is finding what any method would find.

The last of those is testable today without the canvas, and it is next.

Who is doing this and why?

Walter Wilkinson, Worland, Wyoming. This is the sister strand to the Biliteral Cipher work published alongside it.

The reason it has merit is that the artefact is decaying in storage and no one has ever measured it. Owen's five volumes have been argued about for 130 years on the strength of what he said he did. The object that would settle what he actually did is sitting in a crate, and the photograph everybody cites turns out — on measurement — to be showing them the fabric.

The wider project lives at New Gorhambury, which collects the sources, the experiments and the Word Cipher work. Worth knowing before you read it: those pages argue a conclusion. These reports deliberately do not. The difference is the point of publishing them daily.

Technical colophon

Everything below was produced on 4 September 2026 from one published photograph, 300 × 225 pixels, JPEG. No new image was obtained and none was needed to reach the result. Every step runs offline in seconds.

The subject

source     Carla Groenewegen, "Dr Orville Owen's Cipher Wheel: Past, Present and Future"
           Baconiana Vol. 1 No. 6, June 2017 · The Francis Bacon Society
image      "A marked-up section of the canvas"  ·  300 x 225 px  ·  JPEG, 14,046 bytes
coverage   roughly eight pasted pages of about a thousand — under 1% of the canvas
density    about 8,400 px per pasted page

This is the entire published resolution. It is not a crop of something larger that we hold. The file name carries WordPress's -300x225 size suffix, which means the Society's own upload is probably larger — an open lead, and the cheapest possible next step.

Colour is measured against the canvas, not against white

The canvas is aged and yellow, so absolute hue is meaningless. Converted to CIELAB; the paper reference is the median a*, b* of the brightest quartile of pixels; every subsequent chroma is the distance from that reference, so "coloured" means coloured relative to this canvas.

paper reference   a* -0.94   b* +1.34   (a warm, slightly yellow ground)
frame chroma      mean C* 3.04   p99 12.69   max 19.43
strongest 400 px  mean C* 14.5

⚑ The lines, found without a human hand

Luminance stretched to the 1st–99th percentile, upsampled 3×, Gaussian σ1.2, Canny (σ2.2, thresholds 0.04/0.10), then probabilistic Hough with a minimum length of 70 px and a gap tolerance of 6. Nothing was traced or nudged.

lines found            89
  horizontal (0-10, 170-180 deg)   54    page joins, column rules, text-block edges
  vertical  (80-100 deg)           10    page edges, rules
  DIAGONAL  (125-145 deg)          14    a parallel family, spread across the frame
  diagonal  (35-55 deg)             2
  other                             9

Fourteen lines inside one 20° band, distributed over the whole image, crossing pasted-page joins and column rules without deflecting. Visually they are the most striking thing in the photograph.

⚑ And they carry no colour

Owen marked in coloured pencil. So the test is direct: sample chroma along each detected line and compare it with the same measurement on lines drawn at random at the same angle in the same picture.

                          mean C*      mean L*      n
detected diagonals          2.19         50.4       14
random lines, same angle    2.68         52.1     3366
the frame's coloured px    14.50          —        400

P(random line chroma >= detected mean) = 0.43
P(random line darker than detected)    = 0.30

They are no more coloured than blank canvas and barely darker than their surroundings. They are not pencil. A parallel family of diagonals spanning a hanging textile, indifferent to what is pasted on it, is drape — creases in the fabric.

Correction to our own material. An exhibit sheet prepared here in August 2026 stated that this photograph's coloured markings are "in roughly six colors" and are "the primary data of the cipher method." The colour count is not recoverable from this image and is withdrawn. Anyone who has read the dominant diagonals here as Owen's pencilling — and that includes this project — has been reading the canvas, not the marking.

The negative control

A detector that finds long lines in any dense letterpress would prove nothing. Three crops of the 1623 First Folio — unmarked, comparable type density — were reduced to 300 × 225 and re-compressed at the same quality, then put through the identical pipeline.

the canvas photograph        89 lines
Folio control A               0
Folio control B               2
Folio control C               2

The pipeline is not inventing structure. The canvas photograph genuinely contains long linear features that a plain page of the same period and density does not.

What coloured marking does survive

Restricting to the interior of the canvas area (the frame edges and the table beneath are excluded) and separating by hue direction:

warm / red-orange     C*>6  : 42 px, one blob    C*>7 : nothing
blue / violet         C*>7  : 172 px, 4 blobs    C*>9 : 44 px, 4 blobs
yellow-green          C*>7  : 614 px            — but this is the direction of the
                                                  canvas's OWN tint and cannot be
                                                  separated from it here

Two blue-violet blobs sit firmly inside the image at x 108–123, y 30–38 — together a short angled stroke about 15 px long, roughly 5% of the frame width. That is one mark, in an image covering eight pages. The warm-coloured marks visible under chroma amplification do not survive thresholding; they are compression noise.

What Owen's own procedure predicts, and what cannot yet be tested

The documented instruction is to "pencil around every sentence containing the guide word being used, thus enclosing the keys as well", and Groenewegen describes words and phrases as encircled in coloured pencil. That predicts short closed loops confined within a column. Whether the marks on this canvas are enclosures, underlines, or something else is not answerable at 300 × 225, and this report does not answer it. It is the first thing a scan would settle.

The eyewitness, and its limits

J. B. Millett (1853–1938), Harvard, founder of the J. B. Millet Company of Boston, visited Owen's Detroit workshop at least three times.

            left to work it himself. Copied about 100 lines from various parts of the
            wheel and assembled them "without adding a word". Owen had independently
            pre-written his own version. "The two copies corresponded almost exactly,
            and the differences proved to be slight errors in copying" — Millett's own.
c.1894-95   second visit, not yet detailed from source.
Dec 1895    admitted to the workshop and left alone for several hours. Went through a
            pile of some 2,000 foolscap sheets and satisfied himself they contained
            passages from the Iliad — a judgment he was equipped to make.

Named, traceable, professionally reputable, and he performed the operation himself. Against that: he was a sympathiser, the conditions were unrecorded, and a procedure with sufficient latitude can be taught to reproduce an expected answer. The exact issue and date of the article carrying these accounts is not yet pinned down — it is a later, separate piece from the April 1895 "Dr. Owen's Cipher Method". That gap is stated rather than glossed.

The scanner, and why it is shaped the way it is

The failing glue drives the whole design. A canvas whose pages are crumbling can be unwound gently once and never re-spooled, so the instrument goes to the artefact and the artefact does not move.

geometry    48 in canvas width, matched by the gantry span
motion      camera traverses on a strut-channel carriage; the canvas stays put
platen      flattening platen applies its own weight only — no clamping, no tension
lighting    raking + diffuse pair, colour-managed, specified for faded pencil on

Standards this series holds itself to

  • Predictions and falsifiers are written down before the data is looked at.
  • Only open, re-fetchable data. No privileged access, no unique artefact.
  • A determinate procedure. No step in the measurement above depends on the practised eye.
  • Negative controls must return null. A pipeline that finds structure everywhere has found nothing.
  • The null gets published in the same terms as a positive.
  • Where the work cuts against the claim — or against something this project itself published — it is said here first.
