One-Day Report · the format, explaining itself

What this page is

A way to publish one day's work so that three different readers each get what they came for.
This page is not a description of the format. It is the format, with itself as the subject — so every feature below is demonstrated by the thing you are reading.

a working demonstration · nothing here needs a server, a database, or a login
A  Plain answers

Start here if you have never seen one of these. Ten questions, no jargon. Nothing in this column assumes you know anything about the subject or about how the page is built.

What is a One-Day Report?

A single web page that records what one person did on one day of a piece of work — what was tried, what was found, and what failed — published the same day.

It is not a blog post and not a paper. A blog post argues; a paper waits until the work is finished. This sits in between: it is a dated, citable record of a day, written while the day is fresh, and never revised afterwards.

Why one day?

Because a day is a unit a person can actually finish. Work that is written up weekly gets compressed and tidied; work written up at the end gets rewritten to match the conclusion. A day is short enough that you still remember the dead ends.

It also fixes the hardest problem in keeping a research record: knowing when to write. The answer is: when the day ends.

Who is it for?

Three readers, which is why there are three columns.

  • The newcomer, who wants to know what this is and whether it matters. Column A.
  • The checker, who wants to see your sources before deciding whether to believe you. Column B.
  • The specialist, who wants the method, the numbers and the failures. Column C.

Most formats serve one of those and make the other two work for it. This one refuses to choose.

Why three columns instead of one long page?

Because the three readers want the same day described at three different depths, and interleaving them serves nobody. A newcomer scrolling past a table of measurements stops reading. A specialist scrolling past an explanation of basic terms also stops reading.

Side by side, each reader picks a lane and stays in it — and can glance sideways when curious. On a phone the three become three buttons, one at a time.

What is the middle column for?

Every source, previewed. When column A or column C cites something, the citation1 is a small numbered marker; clicking it moves you to a card in the middle that tells you what the source is, who made it, and what you would find if you went there.

The actual link is the last thing in the card, deliberately. By the time you reach it you already know whether it is worth the trip.

Why not just put the links at the bottom, like footnotes?

Because nobody reads the bottom of a page, and a bare link tells you nothing. A list of twenty URLs is a list of twenty decisions you cannot make without opening twenty tabs.

A preview turns each one into a decision you can make in five seconds: this is a government PDF from 2024 about X, and the part that matters is on page 3. Most readers stop there, satisfied. That is the point.

What happens when a link dies?

Links rot. Government pages move, archives reorganise, companies go under. A report that quietly turns into a page of dead ends is worse than no report.

So the page checks its own links2. When one is found dead, the link is disabled rather than removed — you can still see where it pointed, and the preview still tells you what was there — and a dated note appears at the foot of the middle column saying when the break was noticed.

Nothing is deleted. A reader a decade from now can see exactly what was cited and that it was live when the report was written.

Does it need a server, a database, or a content system?

No. Each report is one file. It has no back end, no database, no comment system, no analytics and no cookies. You can save it to a memory stick and it still works.

That is not minimalism for its own sake. It means nothing about the report can be withdrawn by a service that changes its mind.

What does it cost to run?

About a dollar a year — the price of the domain name. The hosting is free3 and the security certificate is free4.

That number is a design constraint, not a boast. A record that costs nothing to keep is a record that will still be there when the interest has gone.

Can I use this format for my own work?

Yes. It is a single HTML file: copy the most recent one, replace the content, publish. There is nothing to install and nothing to buy.

It suits anything with a daily rhythm and an audience that ranges from curious to expert — a research project, a restoration, a legal or planning fight, a long build, a field survey. Two live examples are cited in the middle column56.

I have never used GitHub. Can I still publish one of these?

Yes, and you never have to see a command line. You need two free things and about twenty minutes, once.

  1. A GitHub account3 — an email address and a password. Free.
  2. GitHub Desktop9, a normal application for Mac or Windows. Free. It puts a folder on your computer that is joined to the internet.

After that the whole publishing routine is: edit the file, open GitHub Desktop, type one line saying what you changed, click Commit, then click Push origin. The page is live about a minute later.

There are exactly two words to learn. Commit means “save this version, with a note about what changed.” Push means “send it to the internet.” Everything else in that application can be ignored for as long as you like.

The part people expect to be hard — the website, the address, the padlock in the browser bar — is the part you never touch. Column C has the twenty-minute setup written out step by step.

The one rule that matters. The failures get published in the same words as the successes. A format that only records what worked is advertising. Column C of a real report always contains at least one thing that went wrong — and often a correction to an earlier report.

C  How it works

Concepts first, mechanics last. A reader who stops a third of the way down should still have learned something whole.

The shape

One HTML file per report. No framework, no build step, no dependencies except two web fonts, and those degrade to system serif and system mono if they fail to load. The file is typically 40–60 KB and renders before a spinner would have appeared.

Three columns on a wide screen, three buttons on a narrow one, and one continuous document when printed. The same markup serves all three — nothing is duplicated, because duplicated content is content that will drift.

Why the reference column sits in the middle

Physical position carries meaning. A source column on the far right reads as an appendix; at the bottom it reads as an afterthought. Between the plain answers and the technical detail, it reads as what it is: the common evidence that both other columns rest on.

It also means that whichever column you are reading, the citation is one short glance away rather than one long scroll.

The anatomy of a reference card

Fixed order, every time, so a reader learns the shape once7:

1  number and kind      what sort of thing this is
2  title                what it is called
3  provenance           who made it, when, under what licence
4  preview              what you would find if you went there
5  why it is cited      what it is doing in THIS report
6  the link             last, and only last

Items 4 and 5 are the ones that do the work, and they are the two that a bare footnote never provides. Item 5 in particular is the honest one: it forces the writer to say what the source is actually supporting, which is where padded citation lists fall apart.

Link health, done honestly

The obvious implementation is wrong. A browser cannot read the status of a cross-origin request: with mode:'no-cors' the response comes back opaque, and response.status is 0 whether the page is healthy, missing or on fire2. Any in-page checker built that way reports false deaths, which is worse than not checking.

So the check runs elsewhere, on a schedule, and writes a small file the page reads at load:

links.json
{ "checked": "2026-09-10",
  "dead":   { "r4": "2026-08-02" },
  "note":   "one source unreachable since 2 August 2026" }

At load the page fetches that file — same origin, so the status is readable — and for any id listed as dead it disables the link, strikes it through, and writes the dated note at the foot of column B. If the file is missing or unreadable the page does nothing at all: no file means no claim, never a false alarm.

The principle, which generalises. A feature that fails silently in the direction of a false positive is worse than an absent feature. The page would rather say nothing about a link than say something untrue about it.

What is deliberately absent

  • No analytics, no trackers, no cookies. Nothing to consent to, so no consent banner.
  • No comments. A dated record of one day is not a conversation.
  • No revision. Issues are superseded, never edited. A link sent today resolves to the same words next year.
  • No browser storage. Even the dark-mode setting lives in memory only, so the page behaves identically in a private window.

Typography and layout

Body text at 17 px with a 1.62 line height, measure capped near 62 characters. Figure captions are set at 22 px — larger than the body, deliberately, because a caption is often the only thing a scanning reader reads.

Colour is carried by three accents only: green for structure, gold for the column keys, red for citations and for anything broken. Dark mode redefines eight custom properties and nothing else.

Print and PDF

“Save as PDF” calls the browser's own print dialogue. Print rules unhide all three columns, expand every collapsed answer, drop the navigation, and start each column on a fresh page — so the printed artefact is a linear document in the order A, B, C, and the reader of a PDF is not missing anything the reader of the page could see.

Accessibility

The buttons are a real tab set: role="tablist", aria-selected, panels labelled by their button, arrow-key navigation8. Answers are native <details> elements, so they are searchable by the browser's own find, and work with JavaScript disabled.

With JavaScript off entirely the page still reads: all three columns render, all answers open, nothing is lost but the switching.

Publishing, for someone who has used neither GitHub nor GitHub Desktop

This is the section that decides whether the format spreads. It is written for a reader who has never done any of it, and it assumes no command line at any point.

Once, at the start — about twenty minutes

1  Make a free GitHub account.

2  Make a new repository. Name it whatever the site is called.
   Tick "Public". A repository is a folder that lives on their
   servers and keeps every version of everything in it.

3  Install GitHub Desktop and sign in with that account.
   File > Clone repository > pick the one you just made,
   and choose where it should sit on your computer.
   You now have an ordinary folder that is joined to GitHub.

4  Put index.html in that folder.

5  On github.com, in the repository: Settings > Pages.
   Set the source to the main branch, root folder. Save.
   Your site is now live at username.github.io/reponame.

6  OPTIONAL, and this is the only step that costs money.
   Buy a domain (about a dollar a year at the cheaper
   registrars). At the registrar, point it at GitHub:
     four A records on @  ->  185.199.108.153
                              185.199.109.153
                              185.199.110.153
                              185.199.111.153
     one CNAME on www     ->  username.github.io.
   Then put a one-line file called CNAME in the folder
   containing just your domain name, and publish it.
   Set the DNS BEFORE you publish that file, or GitHub
   shows a scary red warning that clears by itself once
   the records propagate.

7  Wait for the padlock. A few minutes after the domain
   resolves, a certificate is issued automatically and a
   tickbox appears saying "Enforce HTTPS". Tick it.

Every day after that — about one minute

1  Edit index.html.
2  Open GitHub Desktop. Your changes are listed on the left.
3  Type a short line in the Summary box saying what changed.
4  Click "Commit to main".
5  Click "Push origin".
6  The page is live in about a minute.

Two words, and no more. Commit is “save this version with a note.” Push is “send it to the internet.” Branches, pull requests, merges and the rest exist and can be ignored indefinitely; a one-person daily record has no use for any of them.

Why this route and not a website builder. Because every version is kept, with a date and a note, by default and without effort — which is precisely what a dated record of daily work requires. The version history is not a developer feature here. It is the point.

Page D — the setup page nobody sees

A fourth page exists and is never linked from the report. It is the setup sheet: the title and date, the standfirst, which image heads the issue, the reference list, and the last link-check result. It writes the values the report reads.

It is not an admin panel in the usual sense — there is no login, because there is nothing behind it to protect. It is a form that produces a file, kept out of the navigation because it is furniture, not content.

Failures recorded in building this page

Per the rule in column A, and these are real:

  • The first link checker was wrong. It used no-cors fetches and treated any error as death, which would have marked healthy sources dead. Replaced by the scheduled check described above.
  • The middle column was first placed on the right. It read as an appendix and got ignored in testing. Moving it between the other two changed nothing but position, and that fixed it.
  • The link was originally the first line of each card. Readers clicked it immediately and never read the preview, which defeated the entire purpose. Putting it last was the single most important decision in the design.
