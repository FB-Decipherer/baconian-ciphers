One-Day Report · Biliteral Cipher · September 5, 2026

Today's claim

Riverbank’s own 1916 photographs of the two letterforms were turned into a measuring instrument and pointed at a single page of the First Folio — the Prologue to Troilus and Cressida. It sorted every one of the eight measurable w on that page into exactly the groups Riverbank marked — having never seen it. And their own alphabet turns out to show no difference at all for four of the commonest letters in English.

findings may be superseded by later reports

The short version

In 1899 an American cryptanalyst named Elizabeth Wells Gallup said she had found a hidden message inside the First Folio of Shakespeare — not in the words, but in the shapes of the letters. Two slightly different italic typefaces, alternating, spelling out a binary code devised by Sir Francis Bacon.

In 1916 her employers at Riverbank Laboratories published a manual showing, letter by letter, what they held the two forms to look like. Photographs of individual letters, each on a ruled grid, with a written description beside it. That book has sat on the internet, free, for years.

Today's result in one sentence. Those hundred-year-old photographs were turned into a measuring instrument and pointed at the Prologue to Troilus and Cressida — and it sorted every one of the eight measurable w correctly, into exactly the groups Riverbank marked, having never seen that page before.
The two sources, in full, free. Everything on this page comes from two documents anyone can open right now. The Keys for Deciphering the Greatest Work of Sir Francis Bacon (Riverbank Laboratories, 1916) — the manual that shows, letter by letter, what Riverbank held the two forms to look like; the exemplar plates used here are its pp. 38–72, and the table of borrowed letters is its p. 79. And the Bodleian’s photograph of First Folio, Arch. G c.7 — specifically the leaf carrying the Prologue to Troilus and Cressida, which is the one page tested throughout. Every glyph measured here was cut from it.

Questions

What is the biliteral cipher?

Sir Francis Bacon described it in 1623. Every letter of the alphabet becomes a group of five symbols, each symbol either an a or a b — binary code, three centuries before binary code.

The two symbols need not be letters at all. They can be two founts of type so similar that a reader notices nothing. Print an innocent passage in an alternating mixture of the two, and the shapes of the letters carry a second message underneath the first.

What was done today, in plain terms?

Riverbank's manual contains photographs of each letter in both of its claimed forms. Take one such pair, lay the two photographs on top of each other, and ask where they disagree.

Most of the letter is the same in both — that grey area tells you nothing, so throw it away. What is left is two stencils: the ink only the first form has, and the ink only the second has. By construction they cannot overlap.

Then take a real letter off the Folio page, lay it on the stencils, and ask which one it fills. That is the whole test. No judgment, no eye, no interpretation.

Why does it matter that the stencils come from their book?

Because every previous version of this test derived the two shapes from the page itself, and a fair critic could answer: you found a split and then named it.

This time the two shapes come from a book printed in 1916 and never touch the Folio until the moment of scoring. So do the scale and the alignment. Nothing about the instrument knows anything about our page.

There was one thing left to fix: the plates and the page are photographed at different sizes, so a conversion factor is needed. It was set from six letters — a e o d n s — chosen precisely because Riverbank's own plates show no difference at all between their two forms of those letters. A ruler cut from letters that carry no signal cannot smuggle any in.

And the result?

The five w Riverbank marks a all scored positive. The three it marks b all scored negative. No overlap, no letter near the boundary.

There are 56 ways those eight letters could be divided five-and-three. Riverbank's is the best one. Not among the best — the best.

Five other letters on the same page had enough clean examples to test the same way. The strongest of them managed about a quarter of w's separation.

You keep finding things against the claim. Is this one for it?

Yes. It is the strongest result this project has produced, and it should be said plainly rather than buried under qualifications.

What it establishes is narrow but real: on this page, the letter w comes in two shapes, those shapes match the ones a 1916 manual described in words, and a mechanical procedure can tell them apart without any human judgment. None of that was known yesterday.

What did their own alphabet give away?

Something they cannot have intended. Sixty pairs of exemplars came out of the manual. For four of the commonest letters in English — e, o, a and r — there is no difference to find. Lay their two photographs on top of each other and almost nothing is left over. Their own plates supply nothing to tell the forms apart.

That is their evidence agreeing, from the opposite direction, with the fifty-six measurements this project ran last week and published as null. We looked at the page and found nothing for most letters. Their book shows there was nothing to find.

So is the cipher real?

Not established, and today does not establish it. Two founts is not a message. A cipher needs the whole alphabet, and most of the alphabet still shows nothing — on the page and, it now turns out, in their own plates.

What has changed is the instrument. Until today, testing Riverbank's claim meant reconstructing what they might have meant. Now their stated forms can be measured directly, at any scale, on any page, by anyone with the same free files.

What happens next?

Eight letters is a small foundation. The same procedure now runs unchanged on more letters and more pages, and there are about 900,000 letters in the Folio, every one of them free to fetch.

Three things are queued. Whether the split survives on the rest of the cancelled leaf, which nobody claims is enciphered. Whether other letters separate once there are hundreds of examples rather than eight. And whether the whole alphabet, scored this way, produces anything but noise.

Any of those can kill it. That is the point of running them.

Who is doing this and why?

Walter Wilkinson, Worland, Wyoming. The sister strand is the Word Cipher work published alongside this.

The reason it has merit is that the question has stayed arguable for 127 years purely because nobody measured it — and until about six years ago, nobody easily could. The photographs went online free. The statistical machinery got built for another purpose entirely. Both halves now exist, and no one had put them together.

The wider project lives at New Gorhambury. Worth knowing before you read it: those pages argue a conclusion. These reports deliberately do not.

Technical colophon

Everything below was produced on 5 September 2026 from two free files: the Bodleian's image service, and The Keys for Deciphering (Riverbank Laboratories, 1916) on archive.org. Every number can be re-derived from them.

Extraction of the exemplar plates

Pages 43–78 of the Keys carry photographs of individual letters in both claimed forms, each mounted on a ruled grid. Rendered at 300 dpi; frames located by local variance; the inner glyph panel taken as the bright region that contains ink — a first pass that took the largest bright region instead missed six panels.

gridded frames detected          132

pairs (a above, b below)          63     3 dropped as cross-letter
pairs measured                    60

⚑ The XOR, with the reference never scaled

Both halves of a pair come from the same plate at the same magnification, so they are brought together by translation only. Scaling would have destroyed the height difference, which for w is the whole signal. Best alignment searched over ±24 px in both axes.

overlap = shared ink / total ink, at best alignment

lowercase w (italic)              0.547     7th most different of 60
usable stencils on BOTH sides       25 of 60
stencil on one side only            31
NO discriminating region at all      4   ->  e, o (italic) and a, r (roman)
Their own alphabet, against them. For four of the commonest letters in English, Riverbank's two exemplars are the same shape to within the measurement. Their plates supply nothing to separate the forms. That is the same conclusion as this project's fifty-six null permutation tests, reached from their evidence rather than ours.

⚑ Calibration, built so it cannot leak

One global page→plate scale factor for the whole page, estimated from six letters — a e o d n s — chosen because their plates show no discriminating region. A ruler cut from letters that carry no signal cannot introduce any.

a 2.043   e 2.052   o 2.021   d 2.110   n 2.054   s 1.936
GLOBAL SCALE = 2.047      spread 1.94 - 2.11

Six independent letters agreeing to within 9% is itself a check that the 1916 plates and the Bodleian crop are commensurable at all.

⚑ Scoring the page

Every clean glyph of a testable letter, scaled by that one factor, registered to the canonical pair by translation, and scored as (ink falling in the a-only stencil) − (ink falling in the b-only stencil). Riverbank's a/b marking is used only to group the scores afterwards — never to build the stencils, the scale, or the alignment.

The permutation test, exhaustive

At n = 8 with a 5/3 split there are only 56 possible labellings, so no sampling is needed — every one was computed.

⚑ The registration stress test

The scoring above lays the stencil on the glyph by sliding it only — an exhaustive search over whole-pixel offsets, keeping the position of greatest overlap. That is the crudest fit available. If a result depends on it, the result is a property of the fitting, not of the page. So the whole measurement was rebuilt twice more and the three answers compared.

The second and third builds use partial Procrustes registration: scipy.linalg.orthogonal_procrustes (the Kabsch rotation, solved by singular-value decomposition of the cross-covariance) driven by a trimmed iterative-closest-point loop, with correspondences from scipy.spatial.cKDTree and the worst 20 per cent of matches discarded each pass. Reflection is forbidden by a determinant check; lean is clamped to ±12°.

Why not full Procrustes. The textbook routine, scipy.spatial.procrustes, normalises scale away before comparing. Scale is half of what is being measured here — the two forms may honestly differ in size — so scale is held fixed at the calibrated 2.047 and only rotation and translation are fitted.

A dead step, found by testing it

The second build reported a fitted lean of 0.00° for every glyph. That looked like a finding — plates and page sharing an orientation — so it was tested: take a real w, rotate it by a known 8°, and ask the fitter to recover the angle. It returned 0.00°. The rotation step was inert. Iterative-closest-point on a filled shape cannot see rotation: every interior point already has a neighbour beside it, and the correspondence vectors cancel. Refitted on the shape’s outline, the same test recovers 8° at about 78 per cent of true size — attenuated by the trimming and the clamp, but alive. All figures below are from the corrected build.

w          +2.06        +2.02            +1.98            0.08
h          +0.61        +0.37            +0.31            0.30
c          +0.48        +0.31            +0.23            0.25
r          +0.04        -0.16            -0.01            0.20
f          +0.03        -1.40            -1.27            1.43

Under the strictest of the three, w is still rank 1 of all 56 splits at p = 0.018, still with complete separation:

marked a   +0.405  +0.348  +0.225  +0.497  +0.621
marked b   -0.355  -0.415  -0.632
Two results withdrawn. Capital P and f reverse sign when the registration improves. A property of the page does not do that. Neither was ever claimed as a finding, but both are struck from the table above: they measured the fit, not the ink. w moved by 0.08 sd and is unaffected by which method is used.

Not yet done: the per-footprint occupancies are computed blob by blob but still pooled into a single mean before scoring. Matching individual footprints between the two forms — scipy.optimize.linear_sum_assignment over a blob-to-blob cost — is the next version of the scorer and is not in these numbers.

⚑ Where the stencils came from

Riverbank’s plates carry two different kinds of alphabet, and the difference matters. At printed p. 16 they reproduce Bacon’s own specimen — captioned, in their words, “Spedding’s modernized illustration of Bacon’s example of the use of a biformed alphabet”, from the 1857 edition. That plate is not what this project uses.

The stencils come instead from pp. 38–72, the Illustrated Description of the Typical Letters: enlarged photographs of letters cut out of the First Folio itself and mounted on cross-section paper. Riverbank say so plainly (p. 11): the pages carrying the “I.M.” and “Digges” poems, “A Catalogue”, “The Prologue” and “The Names of the Principall Actors” were photographed from the original 1623 copy, and the letters cut from those photographs.

Which is why four letters have nothing to measure. For e, o, a and r, both “forms” were cut from the same fount. Their plates convict themselves not by accident but by construction.

⚑ The circularity check

Printed p. 79 of the Keys is a table headed Letters Taken from Other Sources to Supplement the Alphabets and the Classifiers for the “I.M.” Poem — forty-one numbered entries giving letter, case, form, source passage, line and word. Anything absent from it comes from the “I.M.” Poem by default. Seven entries name The Prologue, the page tested here:

  #   letter  case  form   line   word
  9     S       2     a      1    Scene
 10     T       2     b     15    Their
 17     P       2     b      7    Put
 21     E       2     a     20    Expectation
 25     G       2     a      1    Greece
 32     ct      1    bb     20    Expectation
 34     R       2     a      6    Regall

Six of those correspond to stencil pairs held here (S, P, E, G, R, ct; the Case 2 T is not among the extracted plates). All six are struck from any scoring of this page. An exemplar cut from the page cannot be used to test the page.

Capital P, convicted twice. P was withdrawn earlier today because its separation swings 2.42 sd between fitting methods. It now turns out its 1-form exemplar was cut from the Prologue, line 7, the word “Put”. Two independent reasons, arrived at from opposite directions, condemning the same letter.

Lowercase italic w does not appear in the table, and its plate carries no source note — only the descriptions “the letter is flat topped” and “first and third strokes on the left extend above the body”. By the book’s own default rule its exemplars come from the “I.M.” Poem — the short verse “To the memorie of M.W. Shake-speare”, signed I.M. and printed in the preliminaries beneath Leonard Digges’s commendatory poem, on the same leaf. That is a different leaf, and a different forme, from the Troilus Prologue. The w result is not circular. Of the other scored letters, h, r and f are likewise clean; c’s 1-form was borrowed from the “L. Digges” poem, which is not circular here but is not the “I.M.” Poem either.

Correction, 5 September. An earlier version of this paragraph called the “I.M.” Poem “Ben Jonson’s verse facing the Droeshout portrait”. That is wrong twice over: the verse facing the portrait is Jonson’s To the Reader, and the “I.M.” poem is a separate piece signed with those initials, sharing a leaf with the Digges poem. The conclusion is unaffected — both are preliminary leaves, neither is the Troilus Prologue — but the description was inaccurate and is corrected above.

⚑ The specificity control

Every measurement so far asked the same question of the same letter. That leaves the obvious objection unanswered: perhaps the stencil is not reading the shape of a w at all, but something the whole page shares — a difference in inking between formes, a drift down the page, a compositor’s habit. Any of those would separate the a-marked glyphs from the b-marked glyphs of any letter, not just of w.

So the w stencil was applied, unchanged, to all nineteen letters on the page with at least three glyphs of each mark — 736 glyphs in all. Each letter was then compared against its own null envelope: the 95th percentile of the separation obtainable by shuffling that letter’s own a/b labels, exhaustively where the sample allows it and over four thousand permutations where it does not. The envelope is wide for eight glyphs and narrow for ninety-nine, which is exactly the point.

The artefact hypotheses die here. An ink-weight or page-position effect cannot hide in a letter with ninety-nine measurements, and e returns 0.01. The two letters that look large — p at 1.17 and P at 1.16 — are inside their envelopes: that is what eight or eleven glyphs do by luck, and the envelope prices it in automatically rather than by anyone’s judgement.

This does not enlarge the sample. It is still eight glyphs of one letter, and p = 0.018 remains the floor available at that size. What it removes is the whole family of explanations in which the stencil works for a reason that has nothing to do with the letter w.

⚑ Setting the plates aside altogether

Every test to this point has used Riverbank’s 1916 exemplars in some form. A different question can be asked that uses nothing of theirs: do the glyphs of a given letter, on the page itself, fall into two groups? That is the bibliographical question in its plainest form, and it needs no exemplar, no scale factor and no ground truth.

Each glyph of a letter was normalised for size, registered to that letter’s own average shape by outline ICP, reduced by principal components, and clustered into two groups by a Gaussian mixture. The clustering never sees Gallup’s marking. Only afterwards is its answer compared with hers, by the adjusted Rand index — 0 for chance agreement, 1 for identical. The chance level for each letter was fixed by shuffling that letter’s own labels two thousand times.

letter    n    agreement    chance (95%)
e        99      -0.007          0.028
t        72      +0.006          0.050
i        65      +0.085          0.048
r        64      -0.018          0.062
n        59      -0.013          0.065
a        58      +0.009          0.077
o        42      -0.018          0.037
w         8      +1.000          0.138   <- identical to Gallup
Two methods that share nothing. Riverbank’s printed plates and a clustering program shown only the Bodleian’s photograph arrive at the same partition of the eight w’s — and it is the partition Gallup published in 1899. The probability of hitting that partition by chance is 1 in 56.

i exceeds its chance level slightly (0.085 against 0.048). With eight letters tested, roughly one such exceedance is expected by luck, and it is recorded here rather than claimed.

And the hard half. A biliteral cipher requires every letter to carry a bit; the message is spelled by all of them in sequence, not by a chosen few. The four letters Riverbank’s own plates cannot separate — e, o, a, r — are also the four this test cannot separate, with between 42 and 99 glyphs apiece. That is not a limitation of the plates. It is the page saying no. Reconciling that with w is now the central problem of this project, and it is not yet reconciled.

Why this differs from every earlier confirmation

The 3 September height measurement, the 5 September shape-similarity matrix and the projection profiles all derived their two forms from the page. A fair critic could answer that a split was found and then named.

Here the two forms come from a book printed in 1916. The stencils, the scale and the registration are all fixed before any Folio ink is measured. And the letter that separates is the one named on 2 September, before any of this machinery existed.

What this does not show

  • Eight glyphs. Three of them b-form. That is a small foundation for the only positive result in the file.
  • Only six letters had both a usable stencil and enough clean page glyphs, so nothing here is an alphabet-wide claim.
  • It does not touch the three-printings problem: Gallup and Fiske worked the Second Folio of 1632, Riverbank the First of 1623, and E. S. Smith the Third of 1664. At most one can be reading type.
  • It does not touch the untested cancel pages, and the cancel remains the leading non-cipher explanation.
  • Two founts is not a message.

Next, in order

  1. The rest of the cancel — "gg3"v, "gg4"r, "gg4"v. Nobody claims a cipher on any of them. If the split appears there, it is a printing accident.
  2. Score every letter with a usable stencil across many pages, so the question becomes a histogram rather than a dot plot.
  3. Gallup's 1913 alphabets against the 1916 descriptions of the same letters. If the stated criteria changed in three years, they were not being read off fixed objects.

Standards this series holds itself to

  • Predictions and falsifiers are written down before the data is looked at.
  • Only open, re-fetchable data. No privileged access, no unique artefact.
  • A determinate procedure. Nothing in today's measurement depends on the practised eye — which is precisely the part of the 1916 method that cannot be audited.
  • Negative controls must return null.
  • The null gets published in the same terms as a positive.
  • Where the work cuts against the claim, it is said here first.
